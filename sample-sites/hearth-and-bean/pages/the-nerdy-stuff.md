# The Nerdy Stuff

This page exists because people keep asking, and because I (Priya) genuinely cannot help myself. None of this is required reading to enjoy a coffee here — but if you've ever wondered why the same beans taste different depending on who makes them, this is why.

## Extraction, in One Paragraph

When hot water passes over ground coffee, it dissolves soluble compounds out of the grounds — acids, sugars, and various aromatic compounds, roughly in that order of solubility. **Extraction yield** is the percentage of the dry coffee's mass that ends up dissolved in your cup. Most agreed-upon "good" extraction sits between 18% and 22%. Below that, you're under-extracting: the drink tastes sour and thin because you've pulled out the acids but not enough of the sugars and body that balance them. Above that, you're over-extracting: you've pulled out bitter, papery compounds that only dissolve once the easier stuff is gone, and the cup tastes harsh and hollow.

**Total dissolved solids (TDS)** is the concentration of that dissolved coffee in the final drink — how strong it tastes, independent of how much was extracted from the grounds. You can hit a perfect 20% extraction yield and still make a cup that's too weak or too strong, because TDS also depends on your brew ratio. The two numbers together — extraction yield and TDS — are what a refractometer actually measures, and they're what separates "good coffee" from "good coffee, on purpose."

## Brew Ratios, Cheat Sheet

Ratio is grams of water to grams of dry coffee. These are our house starting points — adjust to taste, and if you want to nerd out with us, ask for a refractometer reading on your next cup.

| Method | Ratio (coffee:water) | Water Temp | Total Time |
|---|---|---|---|
| Espresso | 1:2 | 93–96°C | 25–30 sec |
| V60 | 1:16 | 92–96°C | 2:30–3:00 |
| AeroPress | 1:15 | 85–90°C | 1:30–2:00 |
| Batch Brew | 1:17 | 92–94°C | 4:00–5:00 |
| Cold Brew | 1:8 (concentrate) | room temp | 16–18 hrs |

## Water Is Most of the Drink

Coffee is 98–99% water, so water chemistry matters more than most people assume. Two things matter most: **hardness** (dissolved calcium and magnesium) and **alkalinity** (buffering capacity, mostly from bicarbonate). Water that's too soft extracts poorly and tastes flat — there isn't enough mineral content to bind with and carry flavour compounds. Water that's too hard over-extracts and can taste chalky, and if it's high in bicarbonate specifically, it mutes acidity, flattening exactly the brightness a light-roast Ethiopian is supposed to have.

The SCA's target water standard sits around 150 ppm total dissolved solids, 50–175 ppm calcium hardness, and a pH near neutral. We filter and remineralise our water in-house to hit roughly that target, rather than using tap water or straight reverse osmosis — pure RO water with nothing added actually tastes worse than a well-balanced tap, because it has nothing in it to extract into.

## Roast Development, Briefly

Roasting is a controlled application of heat that drives off moisture, triggers the Maillard reaction and then caramelisation, and eventually breaks down the bean's cell structure enough to release CO₂ audibly — **first crack**, usually somewhere around 196°C bean temperature. Everything before first crack is moisture loss and browning; everything after it is "development," and how long you hold the roast in that window (development time, usually expressed as a percentage of total roast time) is one of the biggest levers on final flavour.

Short development after first crack keeps more of the bean's original acidity and origin character — this is roughly what "light roast" means, and it's why our single-origin filter coffees are almost always light-to-medium. Longer development trades that acidity for more roast-driven sweetness and body, and pushed far enough you get **second crack**, a much quieter, higher-pitched sound that signals the sugars are now caramelising hard and the origin character is being replaced by roast character. We roast our espresso blend to just past first crack for body and sweetness, and our filter offerings well before second crack, to let the farm show through.

## Processing Methods

How the fruit is removed from the coffee seed before drying changes the cup as much as the roast does:

- **Washed (wet process)** — the fruit skin and pulp are mechanically removed and the beans are fermented briefly in water to strip the remaining mucilage, then washed and dried. This gives the cleanest, most transparent cup — you taste the bean and terroir with the least interference. Most of our Kenyan and Rwandan lots are washed.
- **Natural (dry process)** — the whole cherry is dried intact, with the seed fermenting inside the fruit for one to four weeks. This produces heavier body and pronounced fruit-forward, sometimes wine-like or fermented notes. Several of our Ethiopian lots are natural-processed.
- **Honey process** — a middle path: the skin is removed but some or all of the sticky mucilage ("honey") is left on during drying. Named by how much mucilage remains — white, yellow, red, or black honey, roughly least to most. Produces a cup between washed clarity and natural fruitiness.

## Decaf Isn't Cheating

Our house decaf goes through the **Swiss Water Process**, which removes caffeine using only water, temperature, and time — no solvents. Green coffee is soaked in a saturated "green coffee extract" solution that already contains every soluble compound found in coffee except caffeine; since the solution is already saturated with everything else, only caffeine diffuses out of the beans, driven by the concentration gradient. It typically gets beans to 99.9% caffeine-free. It's slower and more expensive than solvent-based methods, but nothing is added to the bean, and — more importantly to us — it doesn't strip out the flavour compounds a solvent wash tends to take with it.

## Why We Wait 4–14 Days to Sell a Roast

Freshly roasted coffee is full of CO₂ trapped inside the bean's cell structure, and it needs to **degas** before it brews well. Brew it too soon — same day, or even the next day — and the CO₂ escaping during brewing pushes water away from the grounds unevenly, which is why very fresh coffee "blooms" so dramatically and can be hard to extract evenly. Wait too long, and the bean has degassed so much that it's also lost the aromatic compounds that made it worth roasting well in the first place; coffee is generally past its best by 4–6 weeks, sooner if it's ground. We hold every roast for at least 4 days before selling it and print the roast date on every bag — not a "best by" date, the actual day it came out of the drum.
