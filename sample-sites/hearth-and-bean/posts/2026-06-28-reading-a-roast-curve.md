A few regulars have asked what the printouts taped up by the roaster actually mean, so here's how to read one.

## The Two Lines

A roast curve plots two things against time: **bean temperature** (BT), the actual temperature of the coffee itself, and **rate of rise** (RoR), how fast that temperature is climbing at any given moment. BT tells you where you are in the roast; RoR tells you how quickly you're getting there, which matters just as much — the same end temperature reached quickly versus slowly produces a different-tasting bean, because it changes how much time the Maillard reaction and caramelisation get to develop relative to how fast moisture and structure are changing.

## Reading the Shape

A healthy roast curve shows RoR declining smoothly through the roast — climbing fast early (drying out the bean), then gradually slowing as the roast progresses. What you want to avoid is a "flat spot" or, worse, an RoR that flatlines or ticks upward late in the roast: that usually means heat input hasn't been reduced enough as the bean's own exothermic reactions start contributing heat on top of the roaster's applied heat, which risks a scorched, harsh-tasting result even if the final temperature looks correct on paper. A sharp early spike followed by too rapid a decline, on the other hand, tends to produce an underdeveloped, grassy, "baked" cup — the bean hit temperature too fast without proper development.

## Marking First Crack

First crack shows up on the curve as a distinct point, usually around 196°C BT, and a good roast log marks the exact time and temperature it starts and how long it lasts. Everything from that point to the end of the roast is "development time" — we track it as a percentage of total roast time (development time ratio, or DTR). For our filter coffees we're usually targeting 15–18% DTR; for the espresso blend, closer to 20–22%, since that drink needs the extra body and sweetness that comes with more time past first crack.

## Why We Keep and Post These

Every roast gets logged — batch size, charge temperature, first crack time and temperature, DTR, and total roast time — and we keep them because a curve that looks right on paper doesn't always mean the cup was right, and a curve with a small deviation doesn't always mean the cup was wrong. Comparing curves against cupping notes over dozens of roasts of the same lot is how we actually dial in a profile, rather than getting it right once and hoping it repeats.

If you're roasting at home on a small sample roaster, the single biggest upgrade you can make isn't a fancier machine — it's logging your roasts against a curve and your own tasting notes, consistently, so you can actually see what a change did instead of guessing.
