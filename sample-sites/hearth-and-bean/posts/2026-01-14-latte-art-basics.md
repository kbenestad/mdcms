People often assume latte art is decorative and separate from making a good drink. It's actually a fairly direct readout of whether the milk and espresso were both done right — you cannot fake good latte art with bad microfoam or a bad shot, which is part of why we take it seriously rather than treating it as a party trick.

## What Has to Go Right Before You Even Start Pouring

The espresso needs a good layer of crema, and the milk needs properly textured microfoam — fine, even bubbles, poured while still genuinely liquid rather than stiff and foam-like. If either of those is off, no pouring technique will produce a clean pattern; the contrast between the white microfoam and the brown crema is what actually draws the picture, and if the milk's too airy or the crema's broken up, there's nothing for the pattern to show up against.

## The Basic Heart

1. Pour steadily from a height of a few centimetres into the centre of the cup, tilted at roughly a 45-degree angle, letting the cup fill most of the way with espresso and milk mixing underneath the surface.
2. Once the cup is nearly full, lower the pitcher spout close to the surface and pour a steady, focused stream directly into the centre — this is where the white circle starts to appear on the surface.
3. As the circle grows to fill most of the cup, pull the pitcher back toward the rim while gently swaying it in a straight line, then lift and cut through the middle at the end — that final motion draws the point of the heart.

## The Rosetta

Same setup, but instead of holding the pour still, you wiggle the pitcher gently side to side while slowly pulling backward across the cup, which lays down a series of connected leaf-like shapes. It's a more advanced pour because the wiggle has to stay consistent in both amplitude and timing while your hand is also moving backward — most people find their wiggle drifts as they focus on the pullback, and vice versa.

## Why It Takes Longer to Learn Than People Expect

The hard part isn't the wrist motion — it's building a feel for micro-adjustments in pour height, angle, and speed that respond to how a specific pitcher, cup shape, and milk texture behave together, and that feel doesn't transfer directly between different equipment. Most of our baristas take a few months of regular practice before their hearts are consistently clean, and rosettas take longer still. If yours doesn't look like ours on the first try at home, that's completely normal — it took us a while too.
