"Light roast" and "dark roast" get used as flavour descriptors — light means bright and acidic, dark means bold and smoky — but they're really describing a point on a temperature and time curve, and the flavour follows from the chemistry, not the label itself.

## What's Actually Changing

As a roast progresses, three things happen roughly in sequence: **moisture evaporates**, the bean undergoes the **Maillard reaction** (the same browning reaction that happens searing a steak or baking bread, producing hundreds of new aromatic compounds), and then **caramelisation** kicks in as sugars break down under heat. Sitting on top of all this, the bean's cell walls are drying out and becoming brittle, until they crack audibly — first crack, then, if you keep going, second crack.

- **Light roast** stops shortly after first crack. Origin character — the acidity, florals, and fruit notes that come from the variety and terroir — is at its most preserved, because there's been the least time for roast-driven flavours to build up and mask it. This is almost everything we run on the filter bar.
- **Medium roast** extends further past first crack, letting caramelisation develop more body and sweetness while still keeping some origin character. A reasonable middle ground for people who find light roasts too sharp.
- **Dark roast** pushes toward or past second crack. By this point, the bean's own oils are being forced to the surface, roast-driven bitterness and smokiness dominate, and most of the acidity and origin nuance is gone — replaced by flavours that come from the roasting process itself rather than the farm. This isn't a flaw; it's just a different, roast-forward style, and it's why a dark-roast espresso and a light-roast Ethiopian can taste like they came from different beverages entirely.

## Why We Lean Light

Origin coffee is expensive to source well, and a large part of what you're paying for is the specific character of that farm, that varietal, that processing method, in that year's harvest. Roasting dark tends to burn that specificity away and replace it with a flavour profile — bittersweet, smoky, heavy — that's fairly similar across different origins. We'd rather let you taste the difference between the Kenyan and the Guatemalan than roast them both to a point where they converge.

That said, our espresso blend is roasted a bit further than our filter offerings — just past first crack, with more development — because body and sweetness matter more in an espresso shot cut with milk, and because a very light roast can taste sour and thin under high-pressure extraction if it hasn't developed enough. Right roast for the right brew method, not one philosophy applied everywhere.
