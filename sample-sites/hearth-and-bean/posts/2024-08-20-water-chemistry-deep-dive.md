I mentioned water chemistry on [the nerdy stuff](../the-nerdy-stuff.md) page in passing and got enough questions at the bar that it's earned its own post.

## Two Numbers That Matter Most

**General hardness (GH)** is the concentration of dissolved calcium and magnesium ions. These ions bind to flavour and aroma compounds during brewing and effectively carry them into solution — water with too little mineral content (like distilled or pure reverse-osmosis water) extracts poorly and produces a flat, one-dimensional cup no matter how carefully you brew, because there's nothing in the water to do that carrying.

**Alkalinity**, mostly driven by dissolved bicarbonate, is the water's buffering capacity — its ability to neutralise acid. This is the one that surprises people: high-alkalinity water actively fights against the acids in coffee, muting brightness and making everything taste flatter and duller. It's why the same beans can taste noticeably different brewed with tap water in two different cities — municipal water alkalinity varies hugely by region, and it has nothing to do with the coffee itself.

## The SCA Target

The Specialty Coffee Association publishes a target water standard: roughly 150 ppm total dissolved solids, calcium hardness between 50 and 175 ppm (as CaCO₃), alkalinity around 40 ppm, and a pH close to neutral (6.5–7.5). It's a range, not a single number, because different coffees and roast levels respond differently — a washed, high-acid coffee can handle (and benefits from) water toward the lower end of the hardness range, letting acidity shine, while a darker roast can take slightly higher mineral content without tasting harsh.

## What We Actually Do

Our municipal water here in Elmsworth runs moderately hard with fairly high alkalinity — fine for tea, rough on filter coffee, since the alkalinity flattens exactly the acidity we're trying to showcase in a light-roast Ethiopian or Kenyan. We run it through a carbon block filter to strip chlorine and particulate, then blend in a small amount of a mineral concentrate (mostly calcium and magnesium salts) to bring hardness back up to spec without the alkalinity that came with the original tap water. It's a small, unglamorous piece of equipment sitting under the counter, and it's arguably the single highest-leverage thing we've done for cup quality that has nothing to do with the coffee itself.

If you brew at home and have never tasted the same beans with filtered, remineralised water versus your tap, it's worth trying — for a lot of people it's a bigger difference than switching grinders.
