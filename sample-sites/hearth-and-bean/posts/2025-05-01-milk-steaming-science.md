I'm Marcus, one of the baristas here, and after four years behind the machine I've thought about steamed milk more than any reasonable person should. Here's what's actually happening when you steam.

## What Microfoam Is

Good microfoam is milk with tiny, evenly distributed air bubbles — small enough that the texture reads as silky rather than foamy, and the milk pours as a cohesive liquid rather than separating into foam and liquid layers. It's not about how much air you add; it's about how finely and evenly it's distributed. Overly large bubbles collapse quickly and separate, which is why a bad cappuccino has a dry, clumpy foam cap that looks nothing like the milk underneath it.

## The Two Phases

**Stretching:** the steam wand tip sits just below the milk's surface, pulling in air and creating that fine bubble structure. This should happen early and briefly — a few seconds, judged by a distinct "tearing paper" sound rather than a hiss or a slurp. Stretch too long and the bubbles get large and unstable; stretch too little and you get flat, dense milk with no texture at all.

**Texturing:** the wand tip is submerged deeper, creating a whirlpool that spins the milk and breaks up any remaining large bubbles into the fine, even microfoam you want, while also distributing heat evenly. This phase does the actual polishing of the texture.

## Why Temperature Matters

Milk proteins (mainly casein and whey) start to denature — unfold and change structure — around 65–70°C, which is part of what gives steamed milk its sweeter taste compared to cold milk: heat breaks down lactose slightly and makes it taste more perceptibly sweet. But push past about 70°C and you start to scald the milk, breaking down proteins further in a way that produces a slightly cooked, less pleasant flavour and can collapse the foam structure you just built. We steam to 60–65°C and stop — check the pitcher temperature by hand against the outside, not by sound alone, since sound is a much less reliable signal once you're already past the ideal window.

## Why Oat Milk Behaves Differently

Oat milk foams differently from dairy because it has no casein — it relies on added stabilisers (usually gums or emulsifiers) and its own starches to hold air, rather than milk proteins. It tends to foam faster and can go from good texture to over-stretched more quickly than dairy, so we generally cut the stretching phase shorter for oat milk drinks. Almond and soy milk each behave slightly differently again — if your favourite alt-milk latte tastes different at another café, milk choice and steaming technique are both doing a lot of that work, not just the espresso.
